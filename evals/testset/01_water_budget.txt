A municipal water budget is built in four stages. First, the utility estimates
demand by multiplying the number of connections in each category - domestic,
commercial, institutional - by an average consumption figure for that category.
Second, it estimates supply, which for a city dependent on a single reservoir is
simply the reservoir's expected inflow minus evaporation losses.

Third, the utility reconciles the two. In a dry year the gap is closed by
restricting supply, buying tanker water, or deferring maintenance. In a wet year
the surplus is carried forward as a reserve, capped at roughly one month of
average demand.

Fourth, the budget is published. The published version separates operating cost
from capital cost, because the two are funded differently: operating cost comes
from tariffs, capital cost from grants and borrowing. Mixing them is the most
common error in a municipal budget, and it hides a tariff shortfall inside a
capital line.

Two figures matter more than the rest. Non-revenue water - the share of treated
water that never reaches a paying customer - is typically the largest single
inefficiency, and a one-point improvement is often worth more than a tariff
increase. Cost per kilolitre delivered is the other: it is the only figure that
lets one city be compared with another of a different size.

The budget is revised twice a year. The mid-year revision is technical, adjusting
for actual inflow. The year-end revision is political, and it is where reserve
transfers happen.
