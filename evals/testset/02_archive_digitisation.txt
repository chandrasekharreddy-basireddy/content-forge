Digitising a paper archive is less about scanning than about deciding what not
to scan. A typical district archive holds three kinds of material: administrative
records with a legal retention period, correspondence of uncertain value, and
publications that already exist elsewhere in digital form.

The retention schedule comes first. Anything with a legal retention period must
be scanned to a preservation standard - 400 dots per inch, uncompressed TIFF,
with a checksum recorded for every file. Anything already digitised elsewhere is
catalogued and linked rather than rescanned.

Correspondence is the hard part. It is bulky, it is often handwritten, and its
value is not known at the time of scanning. The practical answer is a two-tier
approach: scan everything at access quality - 300 dots per inch, compressed PDF -
and re-scan at preservation quality only those items a researcher requests. This
keeps the cost of the first pass predictable.

Handwriting needs transcription, not just imaging, and transcription is the part
that is usually under-budgeted. A rough rule from completed projects is that one
transcriber clears between eight and twelve pages of clear nineteenth-century
hand a day, and far fewer when the hand is difficult.

Finally, the catalogue matters more than the scans. An archive that cannot be
searched is a warehouse. Every item needs a date, a place, an author and a short
description before it is considered done, and that metadata work is best done by
the same person who scanned it, while the context is still fresh.
