A shift handover fails for one of three reasons: it happens at the wrong time, it
carries the wrong information, or it is not written down.

Timing first. A handover that begins when the outgoing shift has already stopped
work is a handover of conclusions, not of state. The useful window is the last
twenty minutes of the shift, while the plant is still running and the outgoing
operator can point at what is actually happening.

Content second. The information that matters is what has changed since the last
handover, what is currently abnormal, and what the next shift must not touch.
Everything else - the full log, the routine readings - belongs in the record, not
in the spoken handover. Handovers that try to summarise the whole shift are
consistently rated less useful than short ones focused on exceptions.

Recording third. A spoken handover is lost the moment it ends. A written handover
sheet, signed by both shifts, is the only version that survives an incident
review. The sheet does not need to be long: three sections - changes, abnormal
conditions, hold points - and space for the incoming operator to write questions.

The most common improvement, in practice, is not a new form but a fixed time.
When the handover has a scheduled slot that both shifts plan around, its quality
improves even when nothing else changes.
